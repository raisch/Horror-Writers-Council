# AI in the Authorial Process: A Framework for Discussion and Policy

## The Question Is Not Whether Every Tool Is the Same

A complete ban has the virtue of simplicity. It tells members that the community will not host work connected to generative AI, avoids difficult line-drawing, and expresses solidarity with writers, artists, editors, and other workers harmed by unconsented training, displacement, imitation, and the devaluation of creative labor. Those concerns are real. A serious writing community should not wave them away because a tool is convenient.

But simplicity can become overbreadth. "AI" describes a sprawling set of technologies and practices: spelling and grammar checks, speech-to-text, transcription, search ranking, translation aids, market databases, recommendation systems, generative text models, image generators, automated evaluation tools, and systems that retain user material for training. These tools create different risks. A policy that treats every use as equivalent may prohibit ordinary accessibility tools or helpful administrative analysis without advancing the particular values the rule is meant to protect.

The relevant question is therefore not merely, "Was AI used?" It is a set of more precise questions:

- What did the tool do?
- What did the member contribute, choose, understand, and revise?
- Whose work, data, voice, or likeness entered the system?
- Did the system generate language or artistic choices that appear in the submission?
- Was another member's confidential material uploaded, retained, analyzed, or used for training?
- What does the audience, workshop, editor, contest, or collaborator reasonably need to know?
- Can the community enforce the rule fairly without demanding intrusive proof of every member's private drafting process?

A useful policy should answer those questions with enough clarity that members can make good decisions before they post, moderators can act consistently, and writers who prefer AI-free spaces can rely on that promise.

## Values That Should Control the Rule

The server's existing commitments provide a stronger foundation than a broad technological slogan. Any AI policy should protect the following values.

### Authorship and honest representation

A writer should not present generated prose, plot development, analysis, or revision as solely their own work when that assistance is material to what others are being asked to read, critique, publish, or reward. The issue is not that all assistance makes a writer fraudulent. Writers have always used editors, teachers, dictionaries, writing groups, research librarians, accessibility tools, and conversations. The issue is whether the description of the work gives readers and collaborators a materially false understanding of how it was made.

### Consent and creative rights

The clearest boundary is also the least negotiable: no member may put another person's draft, critique, workshop discussion, private message, or protected personal information into an AI system without the express permission required by the existing Community Standards. This includes asking a model to summarize, evaluate, copyedit, compare, imitate, or rewrite another member's work. A member's decision to share material in a Discord workshop is permission to read and discuss it within that activity, not permission to transmit it to a third-party service.

Consent also applies to living writers and peers outside the server. Requests to reproduce a named living author's voice, to create "a better version" of a peer's story, or to use a model to assess an identifiable person's unpublished work raise both ethical and practical concerns. Even where no direct infringement can be established, the community can reasonably decide that it will not facilitate imitation or appropriation as a creative norm.

### Trust in workshops and critique

A workshop is a reciprocal human practice. Participants give time, attention, judgment, and vulnerability. If a member submits generated or heavily AI-shaped prose without disclosure, other members may spend scarce volunteer labor responding to a text under false assumptions. If a critic uses an AI system to produce feedback without the writer's permission, the writer loses control over their work and receives something other than the human response the exchange promised.

The policy should therefore distinguish between personal drafting choices and shared spaces built on a particular social contract. The community can allow more latitude in private writing practice than in critique exchanges, contests, readings, or AI-free events.

### Accessibility and practical support

Some tools that fall under the broad public label of AI can reduce barriers. Speech recognition may help a writer whose disability, injury, fatigue, or language difference makes typing difficult. Grammar or translation assistance may help a member communicate in a shared language. Text-to-speech may help a writer hear awkward phrasing. A policy should not casually make participation harder for members who rely on assistive technologies.

Accessibility does not end the discussion. A member can still preserve consent, confidentiality, and honest disclosure while using an assistive tool. The appropriate policy is to protect the function of accessibility, provide a private accommodation route where disclosure would be burdensome, and avoid forcing members to disclose disabilities in order to participate.

### Labor, training, and environmental concerns

Members may object to generative systems because of the sources on which they were trained, the lack of consent or compensation for creators, their business practices, their energy and water use, or their use in replacing paid creative labor. Those objections deserve direct respect. A community does not need to settle every contested legal claim before deciding that certain practices conflict with its values.

At the same time, a server-level rule cannot responsibly promise to verify a vendor's entire training corpus or make members arbitrate disputed claims about every product. The rule should identify conduct the server can govern: what may be posted, what may be used in a shared activity, what may be uploaded, what must be disclosed, and what spaces will be AI-free.

### Enforceability, privacy, and proportionality

No community can reliably inspect every draft or prove how every sentence was produced. Automated AI detectors are error-prone and should not be treated as conclusive evidence. A policy built around suspicion, compelled disclosure of private process, or detector scores will create false accusations and invasive enforcement.

The better approach is prospective and conduct-based: publish clear expectations, require concise disclosures where the context matters, create protected AI-free options, investigate only specific reports or material misrepresentations, and apply proportionate consequences. The goal is reliable trust, not technological surveillance.

## A Practical Taxonomy of Uses

The following categories are not moral absolutes. They are a way to distinguish uses that have different effects on authorship, consent, and community participation.

### Category A: Administrative, accessibility, and discovery support

Examples include speech-to-text, text-to-speech, spell checking, grammar suggestions, transcription, calendar assistance, search and database tools, market tracking, and comparing a story's stated characteristics with published submission guidelines.

These uses generally do not substitute for a member's creative authorship or put another member's work at risk. They should ordinarily be allowed, provided the user does not upload protected material to a service without permission and does not misrepresent a tool's output as an editor's, publisher's, or community's judgment.

### Category B: Research and organizational assistance

Examples include generating a research checklist, identifying topics to verify from reliable sources, organizing notes, suggesting search terms, summarizing material the member has the right to provide, or helping compare a manuscript's non-confidential metadata to market requirements.

These uses can be useful but need judgment. A model can fabricate sources, flatten cultural context, reproduce bias, and give false confidence. It should not replace reading sources, checking primary material, or consulting knowledgeable people. Members remain responsible for accuracy, citation, cultural care, and legal or professional decisions. Research assistance should be treated as a starting point for verification, not as authority.

### Category C: Generative ideation and developmental conversation

Examples include asking for premise variations, a list of possible obstacles, genre tropes, questions to ask about a character, or a rough outline of an original idea.

This is where reasonable members may differ. Some will see brainstorming with a model as sufficiently close to creative collaboration that it changes the relationship between writer and work; others will compare it to a writing exercise, a prompt book, or a conversation with a friend. A workable policy need not settle the philosophical question universally. It can allow a narrowly defined version for private, non-workshop use while requiring disclosure when model-generated ideas or text materially shape a shared submission. It can also prohibit uses designed to imitate living writers or exploit identifiable peers.

### Category D: Generative language or revision in the manuscript

Examples include asking a model to draft scenes, dialogue, descriptions, transitions, titles, loglines, synopses, or revisions; accepting generated prose; using generated feedback to decide how to revise; or substantially restructuring a draft in response to model analysis.

This is the category most likely to affect what a reader is evaluating. It does not follow that every such work must be banished from every casual conversation. It does mean that the use is material in contexts where members are asked to critique, celebrate, recommend, select, publish, or judge the work as the author's craft. A community may require a disclosure, create a separate channel for AI-assisted experiments, or exclude this category from human workshop tracks and events. The important point is that a participant should not discover after investing labor that the submission's provenance differs materially from what the setting implied.

### Category E: Use of other people's work, identity, or confidential material

Examples include uploading a peer's draft for feedback; asking a model to rewrite another member's work; using a named living author's work or persona as a target style; generating a critique of a workshop submission; feeding member discussions into a model for summary; or using server material to train, evaluate, or improve a system.

This category should be prohibited unless every affected rights holder has given the express, informed permission required by the Community Standards. In practice, a shared workshop should operate on a no-upload, no-model-critique basis because obtaining, recording, and maintaining unanimous consent would be difficult and because a single participant's discomfort should be enough to protect the exchange.

## Why a Total Ban May Be Attractive, and What It Would Cost

A complete ban can protect a clear cultural identity: this is a place for human-made writing and human-to-human critique. It removes arguments about degree, prevents uncertainty for members who object to generative systems, makes workshop expectations easier to state, and avoids the perception that the community is normalizing tools many members find exploitative. For an early-stage server with limited moderation capacity, that clarity has real operational value.

Its costs are equally real. It may sweep in assistive tools that members use to participate. It may turn a policy disagreement into a test of personal purity, even when a member's use is confined to private organization or research. It may be impossible to define without banning tools already embedded in common software. It may encourage concealment rather than honest discussion. And it may prevent the Council from learning which distinctions matter most to the community.

The strongest version of a strict policy is therefore not "all software with AI features is forbidden." It is: this community offers human-only creative spaces, protects members' work from external systems, and does not host or workshop generative prose. That is clear, meaningful, and much easier to honor than an attempt to police every private tool a member may encounter.

## Why Broad Permission Is Also Insufficient

The opposite error is to say that all tool use is a private matter. That position ignores the fact that shared writing spaces depend on expectations. A member who seeks critique is not merely publishing text into the void; they are asking peers to spend attention on it. A contest, reading, anthology, or workshop may represent a work as the product of a participant's craft. A writer who shares a confidential draft is entitled to know whether it will remain within the group.

"Disclosure solves everything" is also too simple. Disclosure cannot cure a use that violated another writer's consent, and it does not obligate a workshop to accept work it is not organized to discuss. Nor should a long, technical disclosure become a badge of shame. The disclosure should be brief, relevant to the setting, and paired with meaningful choices: an AI-free track, a clearly labeled experimental space, or a decision not to share that work in a particular activity.

## Recommended Policy Direction: Protected Human Spaces, Bounded Personal Use

The most durable middle course is neither a complete ban nor open permission. It is a policy of protected human spaces and bounded personal use.

Under this model, the server would:

- Prohibit uploading, transmitting, summarizing, evaluating, or using any other member's work, critique, private communication, or server discussion in an AI system without express permission from every affected rights holder.
- Prohibit using generative AI to imitate a living writer or identifiable peer, or presenting generated work as written by another person.
- Preserve AI-free workshops, critique exchanges, contests, readings, and events as default or clearly labeled options.
- Allow accessibility, administrative, discovery, and verification-oriented research tools unless a particular activity reasonably requires a narrower rule.
- Require concise, context-specific disclosure when generative AI materially contributed language, imagery, substantive revision, story development, or evaluative feedback to a shared submission.
- Allow the Council to establish an opt-in, clearly labeled channel or event for members who want to discuss AI-assisted experiments without placing them in ordinary human-only critique spaces.
- Make clear that disclosure does not entitle anyone to critique, promotion, workshop admission, or publication in a particular channel or event.
- Prohibit AI-generated critique of member work in human critique exchanges unless the writer explicitly requests it and the activity permits it.
- Treat automated detector output only as a reason for a careful conversation or further evidence, never as proof by itself.

This approach makes a normative claim: the community values human authorship and human attention. It also recognizes that not every supportive technology performs the same role, and that the most serious harms occur when consent, attribution, confidentiality, or a shared activity's expectations are breached.

## Defining "Material" Assistance

The word "material" should be defined with examples rather than left as a vague accusation. Assistance is material when a reasonable participant would want to know about it before deciding whether to critique, judge, publish, collaborate on, or represent the work as the author's own.

Material assistance commonly includes:

- Generated prose, dialogue, description, poetry, scene work, or substantial paraphrase retained in the submission.
- Generated developmental work that supplies central plot turns, character arcs, worldbuilding, or structure substantially retained in the submission.
- Model-generated critique, scoring, or revision recommendations that substantially direct the revision offered for human feedback.
- Generated cover art, promotional copy, or visuals where the activity concerns those materials.

Assistance is usually not material merely because a member used speech-to-text, text-to-speech, a spell checker, a standard grammar checker, a calendar tool, a search engine, or a market database. The policy can say "usually" because context matters: a formal competition or a deliberately analog challenge may set a stricter rule in advance.

The purpose of the definition is not to litigate every synonym. It is to give members a usable good-faith test: would silence about this use make a reasonable workshop partner misunderstand what they are being asked to engage with?

## Disclosure Should Be Brief, Specific, and Contextual

The server should avoid a single, sprawling declaration that follows a member everywhere. Instead, relevant activities can require one of a few plain labels:

- `No generative AI used in this submission.`
- `AI-assisted: generative brainstorming; no generated text retained.`
- `AI-assisted: generated text or revision suggestions materially informed this submission.`
- `Accessibility assistance used; no generated text retained.`

These labels are examples, not a demand that members disclose private medical information, every software setting, or a complete record of their drafting process. A channel may need only a yes-or-no declaration; a specialized experimental event may invite a fuller process note. The rule should be announced before members submit work, not imposed after the fact.

For private accommodation questions, members should be able to contact the Council without publicly explaining a disability or personal circumstance. The Council can confirm that a tool is permitted without requiring a member to reveal more than is necessary.

## Channel and Event Design

The proposed channel structure can make the policy legible rather than burying it in a rulebook.

- `#critique-requests` and `#critique-exchange` can be designated human-only by default: no generative text in submitted pages, no AI-generated critique, and no uploading of materials to external AI systems.
- `#first-sightings` can require only a concise disclosure when a post contains materially AI-assisted work, since it is for voluntary sharing rather than a formal workshop.
- `#brainstorming-seance` can permit discussion of AI as a topic but should not become a place to paste generated material or solicit model-generated solutions without a clear, separately adopted rule.
- `#the-research-cabinet` can host discussion of responsible research methods, including the limits and verification needs of AI-assisted research.
- A new optional channel, such as `#the-strange-machine`, could host policy discussion and clearly labeled AI-assisted experiments. It should be opt-in, should forbid uploading others' work, and should not be confused with a critique guarantee.
- AI-free events should say so in the event announcement and use an uncomplicated confirmation at submission.

The Council should decide whether to create the experimental channel only after member comment. It is not required in order to allow bounded personal use. The key requirement is that ordinary human-only workshop space remains easy to find and reliable.

## Moderation and Due Process

Enforcement should begin with clarity and education. A first-time, low-risk disclosure failure may call for a request to label, move, or withdraw a post. Deliberate misrepresentation in a formal workshop, contest, or election of work may justify stronger action because it consumes others' labor and damages trust. Uploading another member's work or private discussion without consent is more serious because it violates creative rights and confidentiality.

Moderators should not ask for drafting histories, private chats, account access, prompts, or medical information as a routine condition of participation. They should not use detector scores as sole evidence. When a report is made, the normal process should apply: identify the specific alleged rule violation, allow a reasonable response, protect the reporter's privacy, recuse conflicted decision-makers, and choose a proportionate outcome. Appeals should remain available under the Constitution.

The Council should also publish anonymized guidance when recurring questions arise. Examples of permitted and prohibited scenarios will help more than punitive ambiguity.

## Questions for Member Deliberation

The threshold should be set through a public process, not by a small group assuming the answer is self-evident. Useful questions include:

1. Should human-only critique exchanges be the default, or should members opt into them individually?
2. Which uses count as material enough to disclose in a shared submission?
3. Should generative brainstorming be allowed in personal practice but excluded from all server workshops and events?
4. Should the server host a clearly labeled experimental channel, or should it simply permit discussion of the topic without hosting AI-assisted work?
5. What accessibility protections are necessary so that the policy does not exclude writers who use assistive tools?
6. What level of disclosure is useful without becoming invasive or performative?
7. Are there types of tools, vendors, or practices the community wishes to exclude categorically because of their treatment of training data, privacy, or labor?
8. How should the Council revisit the policy as tools, law, member experience, and publishing norms change?

## A Draft Ordinary Rule for Consideration

The following is a starting point for member comment, not a final rule:

> The Horror Writers Council protects human authorship, voluntary critique, creative rights, and member privacy. Members may use ordinary accessibility, administrative, search, and research-support tools in personal practice, subject to verification and applicable channel rules. Members must not upload, transmit, summarize, evaluate, rewrite, or use another member's draft, critique, private communication, or server discussion in a generative-AI system without the express permission of every affected rights holder.
>
> Human-only workshops, critique exchanges, contests, readings, and events will be labeled clearly. In those spaces, members may not submit generated prose or use AI-generated critique unless the activity expressly permits it. Where a channel allows materially AI-assisted submissions, the contributor must use the channel's concise disclosure format. Material assistance includes generated language retained in the submission and substantial generative development or revision that a reasonable participant would want to know about before engaging with the work.
>
> The server does not permit using generative AI to imitate a living writer or identifiable peer, presenting generated work as another person's work, or using member material to train, evaluate, or improve an AI system without express permission. Disclosure does not cure a consent violation or guarantee access to a workshop, event, publication opportunity, or critique. Moderation will rely on specific conduct and evidence; automated AI detectors are not conclusive proof.

## Conclusion

The question is not whether the community can discover a perfectly pure boundary between "real writing" and every technological aid. It cannot, and pretending otherwise will make the rule brittle. The better task is to decide what the community owes its members when they share work and labor with one another.

That obligation is clearest in workshops: no unauthorized use of another person's material, no concealed substitution of machine-generated work for the human craft being critiqued, no automated feedback passed off as community attention, and no ambiguity about whether a space is human-only. Outside those protected spaces, a cautious policy can leave room for accessibility, organization, verified research, and openly discussed experimentation while preserving the Council's ability to draw stricter lines where the shared activity requires them.
